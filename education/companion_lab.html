C02. The three-way clinical audit dilemma: naive attenuation, complete-case variance inflation, and discard-recorded loss

In ordinary language: Analysts have three bad choices after an audit: trust bad numbers, use only checked rows, or erase unchecked rows. Each choice adds bias or wastes data.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 30-41 (Section 1 Introduction; PDF page 1-2).

What the passage is doing: Section 1 explains why standard missing-data software does not directly solve the clinical source-data verification (SDV) problem. When an audit uncovers discrepancies between recorded values `W_i` and reference measurements `R_i`, analysts face three flawed conventional choices: (1) naively trusting `W_i`, which attenuates linear regression slopes toward zero and distorts non-linear logistic or Cox models; (2) restricting analysis to the audited subset `A` (complete-case analysis), which discards 75% to 90% of patients and inflates standard errors; or (3) setting unaudited `W_i` values to `NA` and applying standard Missing at Random (MAR) multiple imputation (`mice`, `Amelia`), which throws away the accurate signal contained in uncorrupted recordings. Worked reasoning and R connection: Under classical additive measurement error `W_i = T_i + e_i` where `Var(T_i) = 4.0` and `Var(e_i) = 1.0`, the ordinary least squares slope is attenuated by the reliability ratio `lambda = Var(T_i) / (Var(T_i) + Var(e_i)) = 4 / (4 + 1) = 0.80` (a 20% bias toward zero). See `Lab C02` in `education/run_companion_labs.R` and compare strategies live in `docs/learn/simulator.html`.

Evidence status: DEFINITION of the three conventional audit analysis choices and ANALYTIC errors-in-variables attenuation mechanism.

Do not accidentally conclude: Do not conclude that standard MAR multiple imputation packages (`mice`, `Amelia`) are biased when unaudited recorded values are discarded under MAR; their flaw in that mode is precision loss (wider confidence intervals), not bias.

Try it: If true biomarker variance is `sigma_T^2 = 4.0` and independent recording error variance is `sigma_e^2 = 1.0`, what is the classical attenuation factor `sigma_T^2 / (sigma_T^2 + sigma_e^2)` in R (`4 / (4 + 1)`)? **Answer:** `0.80`, meaning a true slope of `1.00` attenuates to `0.80` under naive analysis.

C03. Data generating and contamination framework: latent truth, corruption indicator, recorded value, and Phase II reference

In ordinary language: Each row has a hidden true value and a recorded value. A coin flip per stratum decides if a recording stays exact or gets additive noise.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 42-56 (Section 2.1, Equations (1)-(3); PDF page 2).

What the passage is doing: Section 2.1 formalizes the two-phase data generating mechanism across `N` patients in `C` clinical sites. First, the latent true biological measurement follows a Gaussian linear model `T_i | X_i ~ N(X_i beta, sigma_T^2)` where `X_i` includes the analysis outcome and covariates. Second, an unobserved binary contamination indicator `delta_i in {0, 1}` with stratum-specific probability `pi_{s_i} = P(delta_i = 1 | S_i = s_i)` (`corruption_strata`) determines whether the recorded measurement `W_i` is clean (`delta_i = 0`, so `W_i = T_i`) or corrupted (`delta_i = 1`, so `W_i = T_i + epsilon_i` with `epsilon_i ~ N(mu_e, sigma_e^2)` across `error_strata`). Third, in Phase II, a reference measurement `R_i = T_i + eta_i` with `eta_i ~ N(0, sigma_ref^2)` is observed on the audited subset `A` of size `n_audit = |A|`, where `sigma_ref` (`reference_sd`, defaulting to `0`) is a user-declared sensitivity constant for reference assay noise. Worked reasoning and R connection: When `delta_i = 0`, `(1 - delta_i) T_i + delta_i (T_i + epsilon_i)` reduces to `W_i = T_i` exactly. When `delta_i = 1` with `T_i = 10.0` and `epsilon_i = 3.5`, `W_i = 13.5`. When `reference_sd = 0` (the default), every audited measurement `R_i` is treated as an exact recount `R_i = T_i`.

Evidence status: DEFINITION of the two-phase Gaussian contamination mixture model and reference assay noise parameter `reference_sd`.

Do not accidentally conclude: Do not conclude that `catmech_contamination()` estimates `sigma_ref` (`reference_sd`) from the data; `reference_sd` is a user-declared sensitivity constant defaulting to `0`.

C04. Priors and collapsed slice-within-Gibbs MCMC architecture in catmech_contamination

In ordinary language: To keep the chain from sticking, the sampler sums over hidden row values first. Then it updates the error rate, shift, and spread.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 57-60 (Section 2.2 opening paragraph; PDF page 3).

What the passage is doing: Section 2.2 details the Bayesian prior specification and Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler in `catmech_contamination()`. For exact recounts (`sigma_ref = 0`), the target regression parameters `(beta, sigma_T^2)` receive the noninformative Jeffreys prior `p(beta, sigma_T^2) propto 1 / sigma_T^2` (or a flat prior on `beta` with a scaled inverse-chi-square prior on `sigma_T^2` when `sigma_ref > 0`). Contamination rates receive `pi ~ Beta(a_pi, b_pi)`, and error parameters receive a normal-scaled-inverse-chi-square prior `mu_e | sigma_e^2 ~ N(mu_0, sigma_e^2 / kappa_0)` and `sigma_e^2 ~ Scaled-Inv-chi^2(nu_0, tau_0^2)`. Critically, during MCMC updates of `logit(pi)`, `mu_e`, and `(1/2) log(sigma_e^2)`, the latent variables `(delta_i, T_i)` on unaudited rows are analytically integrated out before applying stepping-out slice sampling (plus a scale-ridge slice step when fewer than 5 discrepancies are observed). Worked reasoning and R connection: In a standard uncollapsed Gibbs sampler, conditioning on the current imputed `(delta_i, T_i)` creates severe autocorrelation when `pi` or `sigma_e^2` is small. Integrating out `(delta_i, T_i)` analytically collapses the likelihood to a univariate function of `logit(pi)`, `mu_e`, and `(1/2) log(sigma_e^2)` before QR-based conditional draws of `(beta, sigma_T^2)`.

Evidence status: ANALYTIC derivation of the collapsed slice-within-Gibbs MCMC update schedule in `catmech_contamination()`.

Do not accidentally conclude: Do not conclude that `catmech_contamination()` runs in C++; `catmech_contamination()` runs collapsed slice-within-Gibbs in R across 4 chains, whereas `catmech_reference()` runs in C++17 via `RcppArmadillo`.

Try it: Why does `catmech_contamination()` integrate out `(delta_i, T_i)` on unaudited rows before slice-sampling `logit(pi)`, `mu_e`, and `(1/2) log(sigma_e^2)`? **Answer:** Collapsing over the unobserved latent indicators and true values reduces serial autocorrelation between the discrete indicators `delta_i` and the global error parameters.

C05. Marginalized corruption probability for unaudited records via Gaussian convolution

In ordinary language: For an unchecked row, the model checks two bell curves. It compares the clean curve with the wider error curve.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 61-64 (Section 2.2, Equation (4); PDF page 3).

What the passage is doing: For an unaudited patient (`i not in A`), we observe the recorded measurement `W_i` and covariates `X_i`, but not `T_i`. Under `delta_i = 0` (clean), `W_i = T_i ~ N(X_i beta, sigma_T^2)`. Under `delta_i = 1` (corrupted), `W_i = T_i + epsilon_i` is the sum of two independent Gaussians, yielding the convolved marginal predictive distribution `W_i | delta_i = 1, X_i, Theta ~ N(X_i beta + mu_e, sigma_T^2 + sigma_e^2)`. Applying Bayes' rule yields the exact conditional posterior probability of corruption `p_i = P(delta_i = 1 | W_i, X_i, Theta)` as the ratio of the contaminated Gaussian density `pi * phi(W_i; X_i beta + mu_e, sigma_T^2 + sigma_e^2)` to the two-component mixture density. Worked reasoning and R connection: Suppose `pi = 0.20`, `X_i beta = 10.0`, `mu_e = 4.0`, `sigma_T^2 = 1.0`, `sigma_e^2 = 3.0`, and we observe `W_i = 14.0`. Under the clean component `N(10, 1)`, `W_i = 14.0` is 4 standard deviations away (`phi = 0.0001338`). Under the contaminated component `N(14, 4)`, `W_i = 14.0` sits at the mean (`phi = 0.19947`). Thus `p_i = (0.20 * 0.19947) / (0.20 * 0.19947 + 0.80 * 0.0001338) = 0.9973` (`Lab C05`).

Evidence status: ANALYTIC closed-form marginal corruption probability `p_i` under Gaussian convolution.

Do not accidentally conclude: Do not conclude that `p_i` has high discrimination when recording errors `epsilon_i` are small relative to `sigma_T`; when `mu_e` and `sigma_e^2` are small compared with `sigma_T^2`, the two Gaussian densities overlap heavily and `p_i` stays near the prior `pi`.

Try it: Run `Lab C05` in R (`pi <- 0.20; w <- 14; num <- pi * dnorm(w, 14, sqrt(1 + 3)); round(num / (num + (1 - pi) * dnorm(w, 10, 1)), 4)`) to compute `p_i` when `W_i = 14.0`. **Answer:** `0.9973`.

C06. Conjugate Gaussian posterior for contaminated unaudited records and reference anchoring

In ordinary language: If an unchecked row is clean, we keep its number. If it has an error, we blend the model forecast with the shifted number.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 65-70 (Section 2.2, Equations (5)-(6); PDF page 3).

What the passage is doing: Once `delta_i ~ Bernoulli(p_i)` is drawn for an unaudited record (`i not in A`), the latent true value `T_i` is updated conditionally. If `delta_i = 0`, the record is uncorrupted and `T_i = W_i` deterministically. If `delta_i = 1`, `T_i` combines two Gaussian sources of information: the covariate regression prior `T_i | X_i ~ N(X_i beta, sigma_T^2)` and the bias-corrected noisy recording `W_i - mu_e ~ N(T_i, sigma_e^2)`. For audited records (`i in A`), `R_i` directly anchors `T_i` (`T_i = R_i` when `sigma_ref = 0`), and `m` post-burn-in completions are retained only after passing Gelman-Rubin `R-hat` and effective sample size checks. Worked reasoning and R connection: Using the numbers from `C05` (`X_i beta = 10.0`, `sigma_T^2 = 1.0`, `W_i = 14.0`, `mu_e = 4.0`, `sigma_e^2 = 3.0`), when `delta_i = 1`, the posterior variance is `(1/1 + 1/3)^(-1) = 0.75` and the posterior mean is `0.75 * (10.0 / 1 + (14.0 - 4.0) / 3) = 10.0` (`Lab C06`).

Evidence status: ANALYTIC conjugate Gaussian conditional posterior for `T_i` and MCMC convergence gate.

Do not accidentally conclude: Do not conclude that a record with `delta_i = 1` completely discards `W_i`; `W_i - mu_e` still receives precision weight `1 / sigma_e^2` alongside the covariate prediction precision `1 / sigma_T^2`.

C08. Two-phase influence function variance decomposition and the interval-width precision ratio

In ordinary language: Checking only part of a study widens the error bars by a square-root factor. That factor depends on the checked share and leftover noise.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 73-80 (Section 2.3, Equations (7)-(8); PDF page 3-4).

What the passage is doing: Section 2.3 derives the relationship between the Phase II audit fraction `f = n_audit / N` and downstream confidence interval width using semiparametric influence function theory (Neyman 1938; Cochran 1977; Breslow and Cain 2003; Angelopoulos et al. 2023). Let `psi_i` denote the efficient influence function of the target regression parameter `theta` and `G_i = (W_i, Y_i, X_i)` denote the Phase I variables observed on all `N` patients. The asymptotic variance at audit fraction `f` decomposes into the full-cohort census variance `(1 / N) Var(psi_i)` plus the Phase II incomplete-audit penalty `((1 - f) / (f N)) Var(psi_i - E[psi_i | G_i])`. Taking the square root of the ratio of `Var(theta_hat_f)` to `Var(theta_hat_1)` yields the confidence interval width precision ratio `rho(f) = Width(f) / Width(1) = sqrt(1 + k (1 - f) / f)`, where `k = Var(psi_i - E[psi_i | G_i]) / Var(psi_i)` measures the fraction of influence-function variance left unexplained by Phase I data. Worked reasoning and R connection: When `k = 0.27` and we audit `f = 0.25` (25% of the cohort), `(1 - f) / f = 3`, so `rho(0.25) = sqrt(1 + 0.27 * 3) = sqrt(1.81) = 1.3454` (meaning the confidence interval is 34.54% wider than a 100% census audit). See `Lab C08` and explore `docs/audit-planner/index.html`.

Evidence status: ANALYTIC two-phase asymptotic variance decomposition and precision ratio `rho(f)`.

Do not accidentally conclude: Do not conclude that `k` is known without error before or during a small pilot audit; uncertainty in `k_hat` is the primary reason empirical audit-size planning is noisy.

Try it: If `k = 0.27` and the audit fraction is `f = 0.25`, compute the precision ratio `rho(0.25) = sqrt(1 + k * (1 - f) / f)` in R (`round(sqrt(1 + 0.27 * 3), 4)`). **Answer:** `1.3454`.

C10. Rubin multiple imputation combining rules and the fraction of missing information

In ordinary language: Across filled-in datasets, we add within-set spread to between-set spread. A small boost adjusts for using a finite number of sets.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 85-90 (Section 2.4, Equations (10)-(11); PDF page 4).

What the passage is doing: Section 2.4 presents Rubin's (1987) combining rules across `j = 1, ..., m` completed datasets. Given point estimates `theta_hat_j` and within-imputation covariance matrices `U_hat_j`, the pooled point estimate is the average `theta_bar = (1/m) sum theta_hat_j`, the within-imputation variance is `U_bar = (1/m) sum U_hat_j`, and the between-imputation variance is the sample covariance `B = (1/(m - 1)) sum (theta_hat_j - theta_bar)(theta_hat_j - theta_bar)^T`. The total covariance matrix is `T = U_bar + (1 + 1/m) B`, where the `(1 + 1/m)` factor accounts for using a finite number of imputations `m`. The diagonal fraction of missing information (FMI) is `lambda_hat = (1 + 1/m) diag(B) / diag(T)`. Worked reasoning and R connection: Suppose `m = 5`, within-imputation variance is `U_bar = 0.04`, and between-imputation variance is `B = 0.01`. Then `(1 + 1/m) B = 1.2 * 0.01 = 0.012`, total variance is `T = 0.04 + 0.012 = 0.052`, and `lambda_hat = 0.012 / 0.052 = 0.2308` (`Lab C10-C11`).

Evidence status: ANALYTIC Rubin (1987) multiple imputation combining rules for point estimates, total variance `T`, and fraction of missing information `lambda_hat`.

Do not accidentally conclude: Do not conclude that total variance is simply `U_bar + B`; omitting the `1 / m` finite-imputation correction underestimates total variance when `m` is small.

Try it: For `m = 5`, `U_bar = 0.04`, and `B = 0.01`, compute the total variance `T = U_bar + (1 + 1/m) B` and `lambda_hat` in R (`Lab C10-C11`). **Answer:** `T = 0.052` and `lambda_hat = 0.012 / 0.052 = 0.2308`.

C11. Barnard and Rubin small-sample degrees of freedom adjustment for finite samples, clusters, and events

In ordinary language: Old formulas can claim more degrees of freedom than the study has sites or events. The Barnard-Rubin formula caps degrees of freedom below the complete-data limit.

Read full explanation, R lab connection & boundary check

Where we are: manuscript lines 91-94 (Section 2.4, Equation (12); PDF page 4).

What the passage is doing: Classical Rubin degrees of freedom `nu_m = (m - 1) lambda_hat^(-2)` assume that the complete-data degrees of freedom `nu_com` are infinite. When `m` is large and `lambda_hat` is small, `nu_m` can exceed the total number of patients `N`, clinical sites `C`, or survival events `d`, producing anti-conservative (too narrow) confidence intervals. `catmech` implements the Barnard and Rubin (1999) finite-sample adjustment: `nu_obs = ((nu_com + 1) / (nu_com + 3)) * nu_com * (1 - lambda_hat)` and `nu_BR = (nu_m * nu_obs) / (nu_m + nu_obs)`.
