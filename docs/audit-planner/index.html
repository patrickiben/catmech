How many records should we audit, and which method should I use?

Start with linear multiple imputation that keeps the recorded value. On two public datasets it came close to a full audit with far fewer audited records than discarding those values. No tested rule tells you the exact audit size yet, because a planner for predicting a specific audit's interval width failed its prespecified test.

✕ NO-GO

The planner is not validated. The rule, fixed before the run, needed a median error of at most 10% in predicted width at every target fraction in both datasets. The 5% target (30 of 600 records) failed with both pilots in both datasets: 11.3% on ClinicalTrials.gov and 13.9% on NHANES with a 60-record pilot, 16.8% and 15.5% with a 150-record pilot. Nothing was retried or retuned. The calculator on this page is an unvalidated illustration.

Neither dataset is a clinical source-data audit. The calculation assumes a random pilot and linear MI. It is the classical two-phase variance formula, not a new statistical result.

    Reading the result

    The rule. Median absolute error of predicted against actual width at most 10%, at every target, in both datasets. A pass with the 60-record pilot would have been GO and a pass with the 150-record pilot PARTIAL.

    Why 5% fails. Predictions there are nearly centered, with signed median errors of −3.9% to +1.6%. The width of one 30-record audit is itself very noisy. A predictor that knew the cell's median actual width still missed by 17.4% and 16.1%, and refitting the same audit with another imputation seed moved it by 3.7% and 9.3%. Better estimates of k alone would not close that gap.

    Above 5%. Widths at 10% or more were predicted within 7.8% at the median and 20.6% at the 90th percentile. That pattern was not the prespecified test, so any narrower claim would need new data.

    The closest failure's 95% interval, 10.2% to 12.4%, lies above the limit. Refused fits: 236 of 14,400 target fits and 19 of 7,200 plans, all because a category had no row with both values. Source: test synthesis, plan.

    Which method

    Default: errors are modest and references are exact

    Differences on the scale seen in these two datasets, with no gross errors in the pilot.

    Linear MI with the recorded value: catmech_reference(use_recorded = TRUE)

    ClinicalTrials.gov at 25%: bias +0.0001 and RMSE 0.0286, against +0.0068 and 0.0308 for the contamination model. On NHANES the two roughly tied. Generalized raking is also well grounded when selection probabilities are known.

    The pilot raises the gross-error warning

    A pilot discrepancy above 5 robust SDs of the reference, or a recorded value above 10 robust SDs from the median.

    Stop and review catmech_recording_audit(). Consider catmech_contamination().

    The reference itself is fallible

    You can declare a credible SD for reference error, and you are willing to vary it as an assumption.

    Contamination model with reference_sd

    The model's niche is narrower than the synthetic study implied: errors large relative to the target's conditional spread, gross errors that would corrupt a linear proxy, or a fallible reference with a credible declared SD.

    Choices the evidence argues against

    Discarding recorded values. Intervals were about twice as wide at a 25% audit: 0.205 against 0.102 on ClinicalTrials.gov, 0.297 against 0.154 on NHANES.
    Treating recorded values as truth. On NHANES at 25% the bias was −0.0180, about 14% of the target, with coverage 2.2 points below the oracle.
    Per-record error probabilities. AUC for finding which record was wrong was 0.543 and 0.500. Do not use them on data like these.

    Where k comes from
    k is the share of the analysis variance that recorded values and other complete variables cannot recover. Near 0, a small audit loses little. Near 1, every unaudited record costs almost its full information.

            Limits

            Not a clinical audit

            Neither dataset is a trial's source-data verification. ClinicalTrials.gov compares enrollment with participant-flow Started, both entered by sponsors. NHANES 2017-18 compares self-reported with measured weight. No public record-level clinical source-data audit was found.

            Random pilot, linear MI

            The formula assumes a simple random pilot, a random later audit, an exact reference and linear MI with Rubin's rules. A targeted pilot breaks it, and the check for selection looks only at the recorded value, so it cannot prove randomness.

            Not a new result

            The width formula is classical two-phase theory. It addresses precision for one coefficient at a time. It says nothing about bias or coverage, or about whether an audit will find a problem. It does not handle clustered records or targeted audits.

            One cohort size

            Every test used cohorts of 600 and m = 20. Other cohort sizes on this page are extrapolation. Resampling a pool says nothing about transport to other data.

            Gross errors break it

            Linear MI failed in the synthetic gross-error cell. The warning fires, but nothing repairs the plan. Rare errors a pilot happened to miss raise no warning at all.

            Not fully blind

            The test is prospective for the planner's code. Its cohorts come from the same pools as the frozen study, whose tables had been seen. The development split shares their source, so choices made on it could still be tuned to these two datasets.

            How k is calculated, and what this page ports

            With audit fraction f, the expected width is width(f) = width_full × sqrt(1 + k × (1 − f) / f). To come within ratio r of the full-audit width, f = k / (r² − 1 + k) and records = min(n, ceiling(f × n)).

            1. Regress the reference on [1, covariates, outcome, recorded] over pilot rows with a recorded value. If every such row agrees, use the recorded value with zero variance.
            2. Regress the reference on [1, covariates, outcome] over all pilot rows. Records with no recorded value use this model. So does every record when recorded values are discarded.
            3. Each record gets a mean μ and a variance t from its model.
            4. Fit the analysis outcome ~ y + covariates, with y the reference on pilot rows and μ elsewhere, and t added to the [term, term] cell on unaudited rows.
            5. From that fit take s², the weight row w = n × C[term, ], a = w[term] and c = −b[term].
            6. Over every record, using μ: u = D(μ)·w, v = outcome − D(μ)·b, and var = t (u c + v a)² + 2 (a c)² t².
            7. k = mean(var) / (s² a).

            Ported to this page: k, discard_k, classical, the plan table, the answer and every pilot warning that can arise here, plus seeded bootstrap limits (bias-corrected and accelerated) and the predictive range. Different from R: the random stream, so bootstrap limits vary a little from R's. Not ported: the multiple-imputation fits, so this page gives width ratios only, with no widths in coefficient units and no rubin_check. The analysis here is always outcome ~ recorded + covariates, with the term being the recorded value. catmech_audit_plan() refuses data where every row has a reference. This page runs the pool-level calculation on such data and says so.

            plan <- catmech_audit_plan(
  data, value = "recorded", reference = "reference",
  predictors = c("age", "site", "outcome"),
  formula = outcome ~ recorded + age + site, term = "recorded",
  fractions = c(.10, .25, .50, .75), target_ratio = 1.25, seed = 1
)
